When I started my Master's at the London Interdisciplinary School, I expected to learn frameworks. What I didn't expect was how much unlearning it would take first — unlearning the instinct to jump straight to a solution before really sitting with the problem, and the people inside it.

## Slowing down on purpose

Most of my design work before LIS moved fast: brief, wireframe, prototype, ship. It worked, mostly. But "mostly" was doing a lot of quiet damage — decisions made on behalf of people I hadn't actually spoken to, features shaped more by deadlines than by need.

Interdisciplinary study forces a different pace. You borrow tools from anthropology, systems theory, and behavioural science, and none of them reward speed for its own sake. They reward noticing. Sitting in a community centre and watching how people actually navigate a service teaches you more than a dozen assumptions dressed up as a research plan.

## Empathy as a practice, not a feeling

It's easy to talk about empathy as something you either have or don't. I've come to think of it as a practice instead — a set of habits:

- Asking what a "solution" costs the people who have to live with it.
- Designing for the edge cases first, not last.
- Being honest about who a product is quietly excluding.

None of that is soft in the sentimental sense. It's rigorous. It just starts from a different question: not "can we build this," but "should we, and for whom."

## Bringing it back to code

The interesting part is how directly this feeds back into engineering. Accessible markup, clear error states, honest copy in a contact form — these aren't polish, they're empathy made structural. A soft, considered interface and a technically solid one aren't in tension. In my experience, they're the same discipline, applied twice.

That's the thread I'm pulling on for the rest of my Master's, and the one running quietly through most of the work on this site.
