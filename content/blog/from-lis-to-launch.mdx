I've kept a "someday" folder of side project ideas for years. This term, in the gaps between seminars, one of them finally became something real: a tiny, slow, pixel-art planting sim called Pixel Garden — equal parts nostalgia for the games I grew up on and an experiment in building something with no deadline pressure at all.

## Why constraints helped

With almost no free time, the project had to be small enough to fit into odd hours on a train, or twenty minutes before a reading group. That constraint turned out to be a gift:

1. No feature could survive if it wasn't essential — there simply wasn't time to build things "just in case."
2. Every session had to end in a working state, which meant small, complete commits rather than sprawling half-finished branches.
3. The scope stayed honest, because I couldn't lie to myself about how much time I had left.

## Old-school inspiration, modern tools

The visual language borrows directly from the 8-bit and 16-bit games that shaped how I think about interaction design in the first place — clear silhouettes, limited palettes, generous feedback for small actions. But underneath, it's built with entirely modern tooling: TypeScript, Canvas, and the same component-thinking I use in client work.

That mix — old-school feeling, current-generation craft — is something I keep coming back to. It's part of why this whole site leans the same way: technology that feels considered rather than cold, with a bit of warmth and play underneath the surface.
